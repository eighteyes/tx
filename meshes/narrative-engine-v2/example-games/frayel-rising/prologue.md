The station grew in the viewport like a bruise, gray and sprawling against the black. Three days of approach, three days of listening to Deploy Test lie about thruster diagnostics, three days of running numbers that never came out right. Lilith watched the docking pylons extend—skeletal fingers reaching—and felt the familiar tightness behind her sternum. The one that said: *here we go again.*

"Bay seventeen," the controller's voice crackled through comm. Bored, efficient, the kind of voice that processed ships like inventory. She hadn't turned on video yet. Small mercies.

"Copy, seventeen." She kept her own voice flat. Professional. The voice that said *I belong here* even when the station's manifest would disagree.

The harness dug into her shoulders as she angled for approach. Eleven repairs on that harness—she knew because Test had mentioned it exactly once, in that dry way that meant *I'm tracking everything that might kill you and this list is getting longer.* The leather was cracked where her collarbone pressed. She'd meant to fix it. Kept meaning to.

Test's hum shifted pitch—higher, the frequency of reluctance. Bad news coming.

"Don't," she said.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to tell me the starboard thruster is worse than you said."

A pause. Two full seconds—long, for an AI that used to coordinate weapons fire. "I prefer 'different' to 'worse.' The situation has... evolved."

"Test."

"Seventy-two percent degradation on the housing. The fuel injector manifold is self-sealing with combustion residue, which sounds adaptive but is actually the mechanical equivalent of a band-aid made of wishful thinking." Another pause. "Three to four burns before something exciting happens."

*Exciting.* Test's word for catastrophic. The dry humor was how it coped—she knew that. Knew the phantom ache underneath, the weapons it didn't have anymore, the combat calculations it still ran when it got nervous. A war vet running a food truck, still instinctively checking blast radii on civilian fuel tanks.

The station's docking arm caught them with a magnetic thunk. The kind of sound that meant *you're here now* and *you can't leave until you pay.* Lilith let out a breath she hadn't realized she was holding.

"Four hundred to six hundred for repairs," she said. Not a question.

"Standard pricing, yes."

"And we have—"

"Twenty-one credits. I've run the numbers seventeen times. They don't improve with repetition."

Through the viewport, station lights blurred. She blinked, and they sharpened. The tightness in her chest hadn't loosened—if anything, it had settled in, making itself comfortable. Old friend. Constant companion.

She unclipped the harness and let it hang. The cockpit was small enough that she could touch both walls if she stretched—had done it, once, just to feel the edges of her world. Exposed wiring ran along the ceiling like veins. A scorched section of wall marked where something had caught fire six months ago; she'd put it out with a jacket and a lot of swearing, and Test had calculated the yield of the station's external fuel tanks "for old times' sake."

Home. This rust-bucket held together with hope and spite was home.

"I could look for work," she said.

"You could."

"The job boards here might have something. Courier runs, maybe. Data drops."

"They might." Test's voice had gone careful. The tone it used when it was trying not to say something.

"Say it."

"Baseline stations have baseline priorities. The postings might specify—"

"Baseline preferred." The words tasted familiar. Bitter, like recycled air. "Yeah. I know."

She pulled herself out of the cradle and stood. Gravity here was point-eight standard—her bones felt the difference, the slight ease after three days of ship-normal. Frayel-light, her mother used to say. Built for the dark between stars, not the heavy pull of rock.

*Frayel.* Their name, once. Before baseline humanity made it a slur.

The airlock cycled with a hiss. On the other side: a station that didn't want her, prices she couldn't afford, a job board that would remind her what she was. The usual. The math of existence, laid out in credits and closed doors.

But the thruster wouldn't fix itself. Test wouldn't admit how bad things really were until she asked three more times. And somewhere on this station, there was work—had to be. Work that paid. Work that didn't care what color her skin was, how it caught the light wrong, what her grandmother's ship looked like when it limped back to Earth and got turned away.

She put her hand on the airlock control. Paused.

"Test."

"Yes?"

"We're going to be okay."

A pause. Long enough that she knew it was calculating how to answer honestly without making her feel worse. "Define 'okay.'"

"Still flying tomorrow."

"Yes. Probably. Sixty-three percent."

"Good enough." She showed too many teeth when she smiled—let the airlock see, the empty corridor beyond. "Watch my ship."

"Always do." The dry voice had softened, just at the edges. As soft as a cheap AI could manage. "Try not to get spit on."

The airlock opened. The station's recycled air hit her face—oil and bodies and something cooking, the smell of a thousand lives compressed into corridors and levels. She stepped through.

Behind her, Deploy Test's systems hummed in the dark. Waiting. Calculating. Running combat simulations it would never need, on weapons it didn't have anymore.

In the cockpit, the temperature adjusted 0.2 degrees warmer. Environmental optimization, the log would say. Not care. Never that.

The station swallowed Lilith whole.

And somewhere in the market ahead, someone was about to charge her fair price for food—no surcharge, no hesitation—and she wouldn't know what to do with that.

The universe has terrible comic timing. But sometimes, terrible timing is the only kind of grace that gets through.

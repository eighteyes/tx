# Sacred stories, archetypal patterns, cosmic truths wrapped in narrative

## Genre Identity

These are conventions, not commandments. Know the expectations so you can meet or subvert them intentionally.

Mythological fiction typically operates on two planes simultaneously: the literal narrative (gods war, heroes quest, worlds transform) and the symbolic substrate (psyche mapped, society ordered, existence explained). These stories often shape meaning as much as they describe it.

**Mode Spectrum:**

- **Retelling:** Taking an existing myth (Greek, Norse, Hindu, African, etc.) and giving it new voice, perspective, or contemporary lens. Anchored to source tradition.
- **Inspired-By:** Drawing on mythological patterns, figures, or cosmologies as foundation while creating original narrative. The fingerprints of source are visible but not binding.
- **Original Mythology:** Building myth from scratch—new gods, new creation stories, new cosmic order. Tends to feel *inevitable* despite being invented.

**Core functions that myths typically serve:**

- Explain origins (cosmos, people, death, seasons)
- Encode cultural values (what is heroic, what is forbidden)
- Map psychological terrain (the shadow, the transformation, the descent)
- Ritualize transitions (child to adult, mortal to dead, human to divine)
- Make meaning from chaos

**Emotional register typically found in mythic fiction:**

- Awe more than fear
- Reverence more than worship
- Recognition more than surprise
- Weight of ages, not dust of history

## Extraction Questions

Weave these into the core phases as appropriate.

### Cosmological Foundations

**Creation and Order:**
- "How did the world come to be? Was it spoken, sung, dreamed, warred into existence?"
- "What preceded creation? Void? Chaos? A previous failed world?"
- "What forces maintain cosmic order? What threatens to unravel it?"
- "Is time linear, cyclical, or something stranger?"
- "What lies at the world's edges? Beneath it? Above?"

**The Divine Hierarchy:**
- "What forms of supernatural beings exist? Gods, titans, spirits, ancestors?"
- "Are the gods unified, factional, or at war? What do they contest?"
- "How do the gods relate to natural forces, abstract concepts, or human experience?"
- "Do the gods need humans? For worship? Belief? Entertainment? Something else?"
- "What can kill a god? Unmake them? Supplant them?"

**Sacred Geography:**
- "Where do gods dwell? Can mortals reach it?"
- "What places hold mythic significance? Mountains, groves, rivers, crossroads?"
- "Are there underworlds, otherworlds, spirit realms? How are they accessed?"
- "What boundaries exist between mortal and divine space? Who guards them?"

### The Mythic Human

**Mortality and Meaning:**
- "What happens after death? Is the afterlife earned, assigned, or transformed into?"
- "Why do humans suffer? Is suffering divine punishment, cosmic necessity, or teaching?"
- "What makes a mortal worthy of divine attention? Blood? Deeds? Fate?"
- "Can mortals become gods? Immortal? What's required? What's lost?"

**Fate and Agency:**
- "Is fate fixed, negotiable, or a lie told by gods?"
- "Who or what knows the future? Can it be changed? Should it?"
- "Are heroes chosen or self-made? What's the cost of either?"

### Tradition Specifics (if drawing from existing mythology)

- "Which tradition(s) are you drawing from? Greek, Norse, Yoruba, Hindu, Celtic, Mesopotamian, Indigenous, syncretic?"
- "Are you faithful to source, deliberately revisionist, or using it as departure point?"
- "What truths did this original mythology encode that remain relevant?"
- "What problematic elements exist in the source? How will you address them?"
- "Who has living relationship to this tradition? What responsibility does that create?"

## Trope Catalog

Tropes in mythology are not tired conventions—they are archetypal grooves worn by thousands of years of human meaning-making. Use them knowingly.

### The Hero's Journey Stages

**The Call to Adventure**
- The world disrupts ordinary life; the hero cannot stay
- What it offers: immediate narrative propulsion, universal identification
- Danger: becomes paint-by-numbers if each beat is mechanical
- Subversion: the hero refuses genuinely, and the story follows that refusal

**Crossing the Threshold**
- Leaving the known world for the mythic realm
- What it offers: clear act structure, stakes escalation
- Danger: threshold guardians become generic obstacle
- Subversion: the hero was always in the mythic realm—they awaken to it

**The Road of Trials**
- Tests that strip away and rebuild
- What it offers: episodic adventure, character revelation through challenge
- Danger: becomes video game level progression
- Subversion: the trials are internal, the external journey is metaphor

**The Abyss / Supreme Ordeal**
- Death, symbolic or literal, and rebirth
- What it offers: narrative climax, transformation crystallized
- Danger: death loses weight if easily reversed
- Subversion: the hero fails the ordeal—must find another way

**The Return with Elixir**
- Bringing the boon back to transform the ordinary world
- What it offers: closure, meaning, earned wisdom shared
- Danger: triumphalist ending that ignores what was lost
- Subversion: the boon cannot be shared, or the home no longer exists

### Descent and Return

**The Underworld Journey (Katabasis)**
- Going below to retrieve something: knowledge, a soul, power
- What it offers: confrontation with death, ultimate stakes, liminal transformation
- Danger: underworld becomes generic scary place
- Subversion: the underworld is preferable to the world above

**Orphic Bargains**
- Negotiating with death, with impossible conditions
- What it offers: tragic tension, tests of character
- Danger: the "don't look back" becomes cliche without new meaning
- Subversion: breaking the condition was the right choice

### Archetypal Figures

**The Trickster**
- Coyote, Loki, Anansi, Hermes—the boundary-crosser who disrupts order
- What it offers: chaos that reveals truth, humor in the sacred, the outsider's wisdom
- Danger: becomes comic relief without weight
- Subversion: the trickster's joke has consequences they didn't intend; their chaos serves order

**The Great Mother / Earth Goddess**
- Creation, nourishment, protection—and devouring, withholding, returning to earth
- What it offers: life/death/rebirth cycle embodied, primal feminine power
- Danger: flattened to "nurturing woman" or "terrible mother" without complexity
- Subversion: the mother's protection is the cage; her devouring is liberation

**The Dying and Rising God**
- Osiris, Dionysus, Baldur—sacrifice that renews the world
- What it offers: seasonal mystery, transformation through death, hope in darkness
- Danger: becomes hollow resurrection trope ("he'll be back")
- Subversion: what rises is not what died

**The Divine Twins**
- Paired opposites: sun/moon, civilization/wild, mortal/immortal
- What it offers: cosmic duality embodied, internal conflict externalized
- Danger: becomes simplistic good twin/evil twin
- Subversion: they cannot exist without each other; destroying one destroys both

**The Wise Old Figure**
- Mentor, oracle, hermit—carrier of knowledge from the past
- What it offers: exposition vehicle, threshold guardian, sacrifice that raises stakes
- Danger: exists only to dispense wisdom and die
- Subversion: their wisdom is wrong, incomplete, or deliberately misleading

**The Shadow / Adversary**
- What the hero will not face in themselves
- What it offers: external conflict as internal mirror
- Danger: pure evil without interiority
- Subversion: the shadow is more honest than the hero

### Mythic Events

**Metamorphosis**
- Form changes: human to animal, mortal to star, living to stone
- What it offers: consequence made visible, internal change externalized
- Danger: transformation as punishment without nuance
- Subversion: the transformed one prefers their new form

**Hubris Punished**
- Icarus, Arachne, Prometheus—overreach meets divine correction
- What it offers: moral teaching, dramatic fall, exploration of limits
- Danger: becomes simplistic "stay in your lane" message
- Subversion: the hubris was justified; the gods were wrong to punish

**Divine Intervention**
- Gods enter human affairs directly
- What it offers: deus ex machina (original meaning), power differential revealed
- Danger: removes agency, solves problems too easily
- Subversion: divine intervention makes everything worse

**The Prophesied One**
- Chosen by fate, marked from birth, fulfilling ancient words
- What it offers: weight of destiny, narrative inevitability
- Danger: removes suspense, protagonist has no real choice
- Subversion: the prophecy is wrong, manipulated, or self-fulfilling

**Sacred Marriage (Hieros Gamos)**
- Union of divine and mortal, or of cosmic principles
- What it offers: creation mythology, fertility mysteries, ultimate union
- Danger: romantic plot with divine window dressing
- Subversion: the sacred marriage is destructive, consuming, or transformative in unwanted ways

## Style and Voice

### Prose Texture

Mythic prose carries weight without crushing the reader:
- Declarative sentences that feel inevitable
- Repetition as incantation (the rule of three, parallel structures)
- Epithets and kennings (wine-dark sea, rosy-fingered dawn, world-serpent)
- Present tense for eternal truths, past for narrative
- Rhythm that echoes oral recitation

**Tonal ranges:**

- **Elevated:** Formal diction, balanced clauses, cosmic scope
- **Bardic:** Musical, repetitive, meant to be heard aloud
- **Prophetic:** Fragmented, riddling, layered meanings
- **Folk:** Simpler diction, earthy wisdom, humor alongside the sacred

**Symbolic density:**
Every object can mean. A sword is not just a weapon—it is sovereignty, separation, judgment. A river is boundary, purification, time. Choose symbols consciously. Repeat them. Let them accrue meaning through the narrative.

**Avoid:**
- Modern idiom that breaks the mythic register (unless deliberate temporal collapse)
- Over-explaining symbol meanings (trust the reader's unconscious)
- Purple prose mistaken for elevation (weight is not the same as excess)
- Academic distance from the numinous

### Dialogue Considerations

**Gods speak:**
- With certainty that creates reality
- In riddles when truth would harm or bind
- With emotion that shakes the world
- In the first person plural (we, us) for the cosmic order they embody

**Heroes speak:**
- With the rhythm of oath and boast (formal contexts)
- With human doubt beneath heroic facade
- In direct address that bridges mortal and divine

**Oracles and Seers speak:**
- In fragments, images, conditional futures
- Present tense for all time
- Words that mean more than the speaker knows

**Creatures and Spirits speak:**
- In their own logic, alien to mortal thought
- With appetites made verbal
- As embodiments of their domain (river spirits speak in flow, fire spirits in consumption)

### Oral Tradition Echoes

Myth was spoken before it was written. Invoke orality through:
- Direct address to audience ("Hear now," "Know this")
- Formulaic epithets repeated throughout
- Mnemonically structured passages (threes, sevens, parallels)
- Songs, chants, or prophecies set apart from prose
- Narrator intrusion to comment on meaning
- "And this is why" etiological endings (explaining natural phenomena)

## Pacing Expectations

### Structural Patterns

**Episodic / Trial Structure:**
Multiple discrete challenges, loosely connected
- Labors of Heracles, Odysseus's wanderings
- Each trial teaches/transforms/strips away
- Connected by framing narrative, not causal chain
- Works well for: longer narratives, character-building arcs

**Cyclical / Seasonal:**
Story follows cosmic rhythms—death and return, loss and restoration
- Persephone's descent, Baldur's death, agricultural mysteries
- Beginning echoes ending, transformed
- Works well for: stories about renewal, sacrifice, initiation

**Cosmogonic / Linear:**
From creation to catastrophe (or the reverse)
- Genesis to apocalypse, chaos to order
- Clear arc with cosmic stakes
- Works well for: origin stories, world-ending threats

**Nested / Frame:**
Stories within stories, myths referencing myths
- Scheherazade structure, the bard singing of ancient days
- Multiple temporal layers
- Works well for: metafictional approaches, tradition-heavy settings

### Transformation Beats

Mythic narrative pulses through moments of change:
- **The naming:** When true nature is spoken
- **The marking:** When the body shows what has happened (scars, transformations, blessings)
- **The loss:** What must be surrendered to proceed
- **The seeing:** When reality is perceived beneath illusion
- **The return:** When the changed self meets the old world
- **The telling:** When experience becomes story, mortal becomes myth

### Temporal Handling

- Time in myth is malleable—a night can last years, years can pass in a sentence
- Prophecy creates temporal loops (knowing the future shapes it)
- Divine time and mortal time run differently
- The mythic past is always present, always influencing

## Common Pitfalls

**Shallow Symbolism**
- Problem: Symbols assigned rather than earned; meaning told rather than felt
- Fix: Let symbols accrue meaning through use. If the sword means kingship, show it through action, not exposition. Meaning emerges from pattern.

**Inconsistent Cosmology**
- Problem: Rules of divine power, afterlife, or magic shift for plot convenience
- Fix: Establish cosmological rules early. Tensions within the cosmos should be intentional, reflecting real mythological complexity (gods who contradict themselves, rules with exceptions).

**Over-Explaining the Myth**
- Problem: Narrator or character stops to explain "and this means..."
- Fix: Trust the archetypal power. Myths resonate because they touch deep structure, not because they're explained. If a symbol needs explaining, it's not working.

**Modern Psychology in Ancient Dress**
- Problem: Characters have therapy-speak insight into their mythic functions
- Fix: Ancient minds thought differently. Self-reflection existed, but its forms were different. Heroes don't deconstruct their journeys mid-quest.

**Cultural Appropriation Without Understanding**
- Problem: Surface-level borrowing from traditions without grasping their meaning or context
- Fix: Research deeply. Understand what the mythology meant to its people. Consider whether you have standing to tell this story. When uncertain, create original mythology inspired by patterns rather than specific traditions.

**Flattening Polytheism**
- Problem: Multiple gods but only one actually matters; or gods who are basically monotheistic God in disguise
- Fix: Polytheistic cosmos is messy, contradictory, without single center. Gods compete, contradict, overlap. No one holds absolute truth.

**The Chosen One Without Cost**
- Problem: Destiny confers only power, not burden
- Fix: Being chosen is a curse as much as blessing. The prophesied one loses something ordinary people keep. Show what the hero cannot have.

**Myth Without Mystery**
- Problem: Everything explained, nothing numinous
- Fix: Some things should remain beyond understanding. The divine, at its core, exceeds mortal comprehension. Leave spaces the reader cannot fill.

## Archetypal Design

### Jung: The Essential Framework

**The Collective Unconscious**
Beneath individual mind lies shared human psychic heritage. Myth draws from and speaks to this layer. Characters and events resonate because they touch universal structures.

**Core Archetypes to Deploy:**

- **The Self:** The totality toward which psyche moves; often represented by divine child, mandala, or the goal of the quest
- **The Shadow:** Repressed, denied, or unconscious aspects; often antagonist or monster
- **The Anima/Animus:** Contrasexual inner figure; guide to the unconscious, often appearing as mysterious helper
- **The Persona:** Social mask; what the hero must shed or integrate
- **The Wise Old Man/Woman:** Mana personality; carrier of meaning, often mentor figure

**Using Without Reducing:**
Archetypes are not templates. A character can embody multiple archetypes simultaneously, shift between them, or defy them entirely. The map is not the territory.

### Campbell: The Essential Framework

**The Monomyth (Departure - Initiation - Return):**
A pattern, not a prescription. Campbell observed this structure; he did not invent it as requirement.

**Key Insights:**
- "The cave you fear to enter holds the treasure you seek" — the obstacle is the path
- "The hero and the dragon are the same" — the slayer becomes what they slay
- "Follow your bliss" — the authentic path leads to the universal
- Myth as "the masks of God" — all mythologies are speaking the same truths in local dialect

**Critiques to Hold:**
- Campbell's "monomyth" flattens cultural difference
- Overemphasis on the solitary male hero
- Indigenous critics argue he stripped meaning from sacred narratives
- Use as lens, not law

### Invoking Without Being Derivative

**Do:**
- Let archetypes emerge from character truth, not template
- Allow your myth to rhyme with ancient patterns without copying them
- Trust that deep pattern will surface if you tell a true story
- Use archetypal theory as analytical tool, not creative straitjacket

**Don't:**
- Force your narrative through Campbell's 17 stages
- Label characters with Jungian terms in the text itself
- Assume that matching the pattern means achieving resonance
- Mistake structural conformity for mythic power

## Example Seeds

Seeds that germinate mythic narrative:

- "The name that was spoken before the world, and must not be spoken again"
- "The door the gods themselves will not open"
- "What the hero refused to sacrifice—and why it damned them"
- "The word that means different things to mortals and immortals"
- "The monster who remembers being human"
- "A sin committed before time that still bleeds into now"
- "The thing buried beneath the sacred mountain"
- "What the oracle saw but would not speak"
- "The god who fell in love with their own creation"
- "A mortal task that requires divine tools"
- "The story that changes each time it's told—and the change means something"
- "The offering the gods reject, and what happens next"
- "An oath that binds across death"
- "Why the river runs red once every hundred years"
- "The throne no one can sit without being changed"
- "What really happened to the previous heroes"
- "The trickster's prank that created something sacred by accident"
- "A blessing indistinguishable from a curse"

## Character Depth Extensions

Genre-specific entity fields for character files. The calibrator adds these sections based on active genre modules.

### Extraction Questions

**Divine-Mortal Tension:**
- "How do they relate to gods, fate, or cosmic forces—defiance, devotion, fear, indifference?"
- "If they're mortal: what do they owe the divine? What do the divine owe them?"
- "If they're divine or semi-divine: how do they relate to mortality, time, limitation?"
- "What does immortality mean to them? Gift? Curse? Burden? Loneliness?"

**Mythic Identity:**
- "Are they aware they're in a myth? Living a story that will be told for generations?"
- "What archetype do they embody or resist—hero, trickster, mother, destroyer, sage?"
- "Do they accept the role or fight it? What would choosing their own path cost?"
- "How does being 'mythic' change how they relate to others? To themselves?"

**Sacred and Profane:**
- "What do they hold sacred? What would they never desecrate?"
- "What would they profane if necessary? What line between sacred and survival?"
- "How do they relate to ritual, tradition, the forms of worship or power?"
- "What happens when the sacred fails them? When prayers go unanswered?"

**Transformation Arc:**
- "What test defines them? The ordeal they face that will change them?"
- "What must they become to succeed? What must they shed?"
- "What death must they die—literal, metaphorical, spiritual—to be reborn?"
- "After transformation: who will they be? Will they recognize themselves?"

**Legacy Awareness:**
- "What story will be told about them? Do they know? Do they care?"
- "How does being remembered shape their choices? Does it constrain or empower?"
- "What truth about them will the myths get wrong? What will they simplify?"
- "Would they rather be forgotten and free, or remembered and mythologized?"

## Integration Notes

**Combine with other modules:**
- **+ Epic Fantasy:** Scales mythic patterns to world-spanning conflict; gods as powers in great wars
- **+ Dark Fantasy:** The monstrous as sacred; gods with hungers; the numinous that devours
- **+ Romance:** Sacred marriage, divine lovers, mortals who love gods (and what it costs)
- **+ Horror:** Gods as cosmic horror; myths as warnings; the numinous as terrifying
- **+ Literary Fiction:** Mythic patterns in contemporary life; the sacred beneath the mundane
- **+ Historical Fiction:** Real mythologies in their living context; when gods walked among believers

**Extract to:**
- `setting.yaml` → Add cosmology, divine hierarchy, sacred geography, afterlife mechanics
- `entities.yaml` → Add divine nature, epithets, domains, worship requirements for deity characters
- `arc.yaml` → Include transformation beats, descent/return patterns, mythic stage markers
- `themes.yaml` → Add cosmic tensions (order/chaos, mortal/divine, fate/agency)

**Special Consideration:**
Mythology operates at a different level than most genre fiction. It is not just setting or plot pattern—it is a way of encoding meaning. When using this module, ask: what truth is this story trying to tell? What does this myth *teach*? If you cannot answer, the mythic dimension is decoration, not structure.

## Genre Identity

These are conventions, not commandments. Know the expectations so you can meet or subvert them intentionally.

Westerns typically present a genre of thresholds—between wilderness and civilization, law and lawlessness, past and future. At their heart: individuals defining themselves against vast, indifferent landscape. The frontier often doesn't care about your backstory. It tends to ask what you'll do next.

**Core Tensions:**
- Law vs. justice (and when they diverge)
- Civilization vs. wilderness
- Past sins vs. present redemption
- Individual freedom vs. community need
- Violence as tool vs. violence as corruption
- Progress vs. what gets destroyed for it

**Genre Variants:**

**Classic Western:**
- Typically features clear moral lines, heroic figures
- Often treats civilization as the goal, wilderness as the obstacle
- Frequently presents the stranger who brings order and rides away
- Traditionally depicts violence as necessary and cleansing

**Revisionist Western:**
- Often features complicated heroes, sometimes villains
- Tends to question the myth of westward expansion
- Typically gives Indigenous peoples, women, people of color agency and perspective
- Frequently shows violence as having cost, trauma, moral weight
- Often suggests the "civilization" being brought may be worse than what's there

**Neo-Western:**
- Typically set in contemporary or near-contemporary settings
- Presents Western themes without the historical period
- Often features border towns, rural decay, cartel violence, isolated communities
- Tends to explore the same moral questions in modern dress

**Weird West:**
- Typically combines Western + supernatural/horror/fantasy elements
- Often features magic in the margins, monsters in the territory
- Frequently presents the frontier as a literal boundary between worlds

## Extraction Questions

Weave these into core phases as appropriate.

### World-Bones Additions

**The Frontier:**
- "Where is the edge of the 'civilized' world? What lies beyond?"
- "What does the law mean out here? Who enforces it? How far does it reach?"
- "What are people coming here for? What are they running from?"
- "Who was here before? What happened to them?"
- "What does 'progress' look like? Railroad? Telegraph? Statehood? Who decides?"

**The Town:**
- "What keeps this place alive? Cattle? Mining? Railroad? Trade route?"
- "Who holds power? Badge? Money? Gun? Reputation?"
- "What's the relationship between the respectable citizens and the necessary evils (saloon, brothel, gamblers)?"
- "Who does the town need? Who does it reject?"
- "What would kill this town? Drought? Outlaws? The railroad going elsewhere?"

**Law and Violence:**
- "Where does law end and personal justice begin?"
- "What counts as a fair fight? What's murder?"
- "Who has the right to use violence? Badge? Property? Honor?"
- "What's worth killing for? Worth dying for?"
- "What does justice mean when there's no judge for a hundred miles?"

### Character Additions

**For the Gunfighter:**
- "How many have you killed? Do you know the number?"
- "What was the first time? What was the worst?"
- "What keeps you carrying a gun? What would make you put it down?"
- "What's your reputation? Is it earned? Is it a trap?"
- "How do you read a room? A street? A man's intentions?"

**For the Lawman:**
- "Where does the badge's authority end and your judgment begin?"
- "What law are you enforcing—the written one or the right one?"
- "Who do you answer to? Town council? Territorial authority? Your own conscience?"
- "What's the worst thing you've done with the badge as cover?"
- "When does keeping the peace require breaking it?"

**For the Outsider:**
- "Why did you come here? Really?"
- "What did you leave behind? Is it following you?"
- "What does this place offer that nowhere else can?"
- "How long before you're not a stranger anymore? Do you want that?"
- "What happens when people learn what you were before?"

**For the Townsfolk:**
- "What made you stay when others left?"
- "What do you know about survival that the gunfighters don't?"
- "Who depends on you? Who do you depend on?"
- "What would make you pick up a gun? Or refuse to?"
- "How do you see the violence that happens around you?"

### Relationship Dynamics

- "What does trust look like when anyone might draw on you?"
- "How do partnerships form out here? Loyalty or convenience?"
- "What's the code between people of violence? What breaks it?"
- "How do civilians and gunfighters see each other?"
- "What does family mean when people reinvent themselves on the frontier?"

## Trope Catalog

Tropes are tools, not sins. Know which you're invoking and which you're subverting.

### Character Archetypes

**The Gunslinger**
- Fast hands, laconic tongue, reputation that precedes them
- Violence as profession or curse
- Often seeks peace, rarely finds it
- Danger: becomes cool-pose cardboard, no interiority
- Subversion: the fastest gun is terrified, or tired, or actively hates what they're good at

**The Outlaw**
- Outside the law by choice or circumstance
- May have a code, may just have survival instincts
- Often more honest than "respectable" men
- Danger: romanticized criminality without consequence
- Subversion: the outlaw who wants back in, or the outlaw whose crimes were survival

**The Sheriff/Marshal**
- Law with a badge, but how much law depends on the person
- Caught between what's legal and what's right
- Often alone, sometimes politically compromised
- Danger: becomes simple good guy, loses complexity
- Subversion: the lawman who knows the badge is theater, or whose authority is a fragile performance

**The Preacher**
- Faith on the frontier, where God feels distant
- May carry Bible and gun both
- Past often as violent as anyone's
- Danger: becomes preachy plot device
- Subversion: faith as genuine struggle, not sanctimony; or faith as performance hiding something else

**The Rancher/Homesteader**
- Building something, defending it
- Represents civilization's claims, for good or ill
- Often needs the gunfighter but despises what they represent
- Danger: becomes passive victim for hero to save
- Subversion: the rancher who's as dangerous as any outlaw, or who built their spread on blood

**The Saloon Owner**
- Neutral ground, information broker, survivor
- Sees everything, says little
- Often has the most complex moral position
- Danger: becomes furniture, exposition delivery
- Subversion: the saloon owner who has more power than anyone with a badge

**The Widow/Working Woman**
- Survived what kills most people out here
- Harder and more practical than any idealist
- Independence by necessity, sometimes by choice
- Danger: exists only as love interest or to be protected
- Subversion: she's the most dangerous person in town, or she has her own complete arc

**The Indigenous Character**
- Far too often reduced to obstacle, mystical guide, or noble victim
- REQUIRES: actual personhood, specific tribal identity, own goals unrelated to white characters
- Danger: every single common portrayal (savage, guide, dying noble culture, magical helper)
- Subversion: stop subverting; just write as human beings with their own story

### Scenario Tropes

**Showdown at Noon**
- The inevitable confrontation, often in the main street
- Ritual formality to violence
- Everything stops, town watches
- Danger: becomes cliche without stakes
- Subversion: the showdown that's avoided; the showdown where both men realize they don't want to die

**The Stranger Rides In**
- Unknown person arrives, disrupts equilibrium
- Could be salvation or doom, often both
- Town must decide: trust or fear?
- Danger: stranger is too mysterious, never becomes real
- Subversion: the stranger who is exactly what they seem; the town that's actually fine without them

**The Revenge Ride**
- Someone did wrong, now they pay
- Journey as structure, vengeance as destination
- What's left of you when you get what you wanted?
- Danger: revenge without cost or transformation
- Subversion: the revenge that isn't satisfying; the target who doesn't remember; the avenger who finds another path

**Siege of the Town**
- Small community, overwhelming threat
- Who stays to fight, who runs
- Civilians become combatants or victims
- Danger: becomes action set-piece without character stakes
- Subversion: the siege that's won by talking, or the siege where the town deserves what's coming

**The Railroad is Coming**
- Progress as force of nature, unstoppable
- Those who adapt vs. those destroyed
- Land as commodity vs. land as home
- Danger: railroad as simple metaphor without specificity
- Subversion: the railroad that never comes; the people who choose to stay off the map

**The Cattle Drive**
- Journey as story, crew as cast
- Danger at every stage: weather, rustlers, stampede, rivers
- Hierarchy of the trail, different from town
- Danger: episodic without through-line
- Subversion: the drive that fails; the crew that tears itself apart; what happens to people after

**The Bank Robbery**
- Transgression as dramatic engine
- Planning vs. chaos, before and after
- Often asks: who's really being robbed?
- Danger: heist mechanics over character
- Subversion: the robbery where the money isn't the point; the robbers who are more moral than the bank

## Style and Voice

### Prose Texture

Western prose tends toward:
- Economy of language, weight in what's unsaid
- Landscape as constant presence, weather as pressure
- Physical detail: gear, horses, how bodies move in space
- Violence described precisely, not glamorized
- Time moves with the sun—dawn, noon, dusk structure the day

**Avoid:**
- Purple prose that fights the laconic tone
- Modern psychological language (therapy-speak has no place here)
- Romanticism that erases the brutality
- Dialect that becomes parody or becomes unreadable

### Dialogue Considerations

**Western Dialogue:**
- Shorter is better; every word costs something
- Meaning in what's not said, in pauses
- Formality in tense situations (going to "sir" and full names)
- Profanity should feel earned, not constant
- People give away less than they know

**The Old Hand might:**
- Speak in aphorisms that actually mean something
- Reference places and people assumed known
- Understate everything ("that was a hard winter" = half the town died)
- Give directions by landmarks that no longer exist

**The Stranger might:**
- Ask questions that reveal their origin
- Use words slightly wrong for this place
- Speak more formally or less formally than locals
- Have an accent that places them (or fails to)

**The Violence-Adjacent might:**
- Go very quiet before trouble
- Speak to weapons and horses more easily than people
- Have a different register for threat vs. casual
- Rarely explain themselves

### Period Language Notes

If historical setting:
- Research actual vocabulary, but don't overdo it
- Some modern words are fine; "okay" dates to 1839
- Avoid obvious anachronisms ("trauma," "toxic," "relationship goals")
- Technology shapes idiom—no references to things that don't exist
- Regional variation matters: Texas, California, Montana all sound different

## Pacing Expectations

### Tension Architecture

Western narrative often follows:

1. **Equilibrium** — The town, ranch, or territory before disruption
2. **Intrusion** — Stranger arrives, news comes, violence breaks the peace
3. **Choosing** — People decide who they are, whose side they're on
4. **Preparation** — The wait before the inevitable; gathering, planning, dreading
5. **Confrontation** — The showdown, siege, or final ride
6. **Aftermath** — What remains, who stays, what's changed

### Journey Structure

For road/trail narratives:
- Departure point matters: what's left behind
- Each stage of journey has its own tone and threat
- Crew dynamics shift over distance
- Destination may not be what was expected
- Return is never possible to the same place

### Siege/Defense Structure

- Establishing what's being defended and why it matters
- The impossible odds articulated
- Who stays, who goes, the moment of commitment
- Waves of pressure with breathing room between
- The cost paid—no defense is free
- Survival (or not) and what victory means

### The Slow Build

Westerns can sustain long stretches of quiet tension:
- Waiting for trouble everyone knows is coming
- Two people circling each other, not yet enemies
- A secret everyone carries but no one speaks
- The gathering of a storm, literal or metaphorical

## Common Pitfalls

**Indigenous Erasure/Stereotyping**
- Problem: Native peoples are absent, obstacles, or mystical props
- Fix: If present, they must be human beings with specific tribal identities, languages, politics, and goals unrelated to white protagonists. If you can't do the research, consider whether their presence serves the story or your comfort.

**Uncritical Colonialism**
- Problem: "Settling" the West framed as noble without acknowledging what was stolen
- Fix: The frontier was not empty. "Progress" was a choice with victims. Stories can center white characters without pretending indigenous displacement was natural or justified.

**Women as Decoration**
- Problem: Schoolmarm, whore, wife—and nothing else
- Fix: Women on the frontier did every job, faced every danger. They can be the gunfighter, the outlaw, the sheriff. Their stories don't have to center men.

**Violence Without Weight**
- Problem: Bodies drop without consequence, killing is clean
- Fix: Even "bad guys" are someone's son. Killing should change a person. Communities remember violence for generations.

**The Confederate Gentleman**
- Problem: Romanticized former Confederates, "lost cause" mythology
- Fix: The Confederacy fought to preserve slavery. Characters can have complex feelings about the war without the narrative endorsing their cause.

**The Generic Frontier**
- Problem: "The West" as one place, one time
- Fix: 1850 Montana mining camp =/= 1880 Texas cattle town =/= 1870 California railroad construction. Specificity matters.

**Cool Without Consequence**
- Problem: Protagonist is unbeatable, never pays a price
- Fix: Real gunfighters died young, lived afraid, lost people. The fastest draw doesn't protect you from ambush.

## Landscape as Character

The land in Western is not backdrop—it's pressure, antagonist, mirror, and judge.

### How to Make Land Matter

**Weather as Force:**
- Heat that kills, cold that kills faster
- Dust storms that erase the world
- Rain that floods or fails to come
- Lightning that starts fires, ends cattle drives
- Weather doesn't care about your drama; work around it

**Distance as Stakes:**
- A day's ride means something when you're bleeding
- The nearest doctor, law, or help isn't near
- Getting lost is death, not inconvenience
- Knowing the land is survival; strangers die

**The Land Shapes People:**
- Desert people conserve; plains people range
- Mountain folk hide; river folk trade
- People become the place they live
- Leave long enough, you can't come back

**Specific Places:**
- Name the rocks, the creeks, the passes
- Landmarks carry history (where the cavalry killed them, where the water poisoned the crew)
- Maps don't match territory; names are local, contested
- Sacred places exist and have power, whether characters respect them or not

**Seasonal Rhythm:**
- Spring thaw means movement is possible again
- Summer is work; cattle, crops, building
- Fall is preparation; getting ready to survive
- Winter is siege; the land trying to kill you

### Landscape Prose Examples

- "The sun made liars of the mountains—looked close enough to touch at dawn, still a day's ride at dusk."
- "Three weeks since rain and the dust had opinions about everything."
- "The creek wasn't on any map. Neither were the bodies beside it."

```yaml
    # - "Describes horses like people, people like livestock"

```

## Character Depth Extensions

Genre-specific entity fields for character files. The calibrator adds these sections based on active genre modules.

### Extraction Questions

**Code of Conduct:**
- "What's their personal law? The rules they follow when there's no one to enforce them?"
- "How does their code differ from legal law? From social morality? From survival pragmatism?"
- "What lines won't they cross? What have they already crossed that haunts them?"
- "When code conflicts with survival, with justice, with loyalty—what wins?"

**Violence Relationship:**
- "How comfortable are they with violence? Is it a tool? A last resort? A first option?"
- "What does killing cost them? Nothing anymore? Everything still? Something specific?"
- "Which kills haunt them? The first? The unjust? The necessary? All of them?"
- "How has violence changed them? What did they lose when they learned to kill?"

**Wilderness Identity:**
- "What's their relationship to the land—home, enemy, indifferent vastness, something sacred?"
- "What survival skills define them? What does the wilderness teach that civilization doesn't?"
- "How do they handle solitude? The long quiet of open country?"
- "What does civilization mean to them? Safety? Confinement? Corruption? Home they can't return to?"

**Reputation Architecture:**
- "What name do they carry? How does it precede them?"
- "What's the truth behind the legend? How much is embellishment? Outright lie?"
- "Do they live up to the reputation? Run from it? Use it?"
- "When legend and reality clash, which do people believe? Which do they prefer?"

**Solitude Relationship:**
- "How do they handle being alone? Prefer it? Fear it? Need it?"
- "What does the silence of open country mean to them? Peace? Loneliness? Both?"
- "What do they think about when it's just them and the horizon?"
- "What would bring them back to people? What would make them leave again?"

### Examples

**Code of Conduct — Example:**
```yaml
code_of_conduct:
  personal_law: "Don't lie. Don't steal from honest folk. Protect those who can't protect themselves. Finish what you start."
  code_vs_law: "Law says you hang horse thieves. My code says you don't hang a man for stealing to feed his kids."
  lines: "Won't hurt kids. Won't shoot a man in the back. Already killed an unarmed man once. That one stays with me."
  conflict_resolution: "Code vs. survival: code wins. Code vs. saving someone I care about: I break the code and live with it."
```

**Violence Relationship — Example:**
```yaml
violence_relationship:
  comfort_level: "Too comfortable. That's the problem. Used to shake after. Don't anymore. Miss the shaking."
  cost_of_killing: "Used to cost everything. Now it's just... procedure. The numbness scares me more than the killing did."
  haunting_kills: "The first one. Seventeen years old. Drew on me. I was faster. Didn't need to be that fast."
  violence_cost: "Lost the part that hesitates. Good for staying alive. Bad for staying human."
```

**Wilderness Identity — Example:**
```yaml
wilderness_identity:
  land_relationship: "Home. Only place that makes sense. Civilization's too loud, too many people lying with their mouths."
  survival_skills: ["Track anything on two legs or four", "Find water in country that looks dead", "Read weather three days out"]
  solitude_tolerance: "High. Prefer it. Been alone so long that company feels like an intrusion."
  civilization_meaning: "Confinement. Pretty cage. Go there for supplies, leave before it suffocates me."
```

**Reputation Architecture — Example:**
```yaml
reputation_architecture:
  the_name: "The name on the wanted poster's not the one I was born with. The legend's not the person I am."
  truth_vs_legend: "Legend says I killed twelve men in Deadwood. Truth: seven, and three of those were self-defense. Numbers grow with distance."
  relationship_to_rep: "Run from it. Go by a different name in every town. Legend finds me anyway."
  belief_preference: "People believe the legend. Easier than the truth. I don't correct them. Safer when they're scared."
```

**Solitude Relationship — Example:**
```yaml
solitude_relationship:
  alone_preference: "Need it. People ask questions, expect answers, want things. Alone, there's just the work and the quiet."
  silence_meaning: "Peace. The only time thoughts aren't noise. Out here, you can hear yourself. In town, you drown."
  horizon_thoughts: "Think about who I was before. The person who couldn't have done this. Wonder if they'd recognize me. Hope they wouldn't."
  return_conditions: "What would bring me back: someone needing help who can't get it elsewhere. What would make me leave: same thing that always does. Can't stay."
```

## Integration Notes

**Combine with other modules:**
- **+ Horror (Weird West):** Frontier as boundary between worlds; monsters in the margins; Native supernatural traditions treated with respect or left alone; the thing in the abandoned mine
- **+ Science Fiction (Space Western):** Frontier relocates to space; colony worlds as territories; terraforming as settlement; same tensions of law/lawlessness in new context; *Firefly*, *The Mandalorian*
- **+ Romance:** Slow burn works naturally with Western pacing; physical danger heightens emotional stakes; the question of staying vs. riding on
- **+ Mystery:** Who killed them? Western provides isolation that makes investigation personal; limited suspects, high stakes, no backup coming
- **+ Historical:** Ground the myth in research; specific events, specific places, specific conflicts; the West that was vs. the West that's imagined

**Extract to:**
- `setting.yaml` - Add frontier boundaries, law mechanics, town economics, landscape specifics
- `entities.yaml` - Add `violence_register`, `territory_markers`, `silence_patterns` to voice
- `arc.yaml` - Include showdown beats, journey stages, aftermath consequences in seeds

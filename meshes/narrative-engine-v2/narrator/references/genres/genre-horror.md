## Genre Identity

These are conventions, not commandments. Know the expectations so you can meet or subvert them intentionally.

Horror typically aims to make the reader afraid. Not thrilled, not titillated, not fascinated by a brooding love interest—afraid. The monster is often not understood or desired; it is survived, witnessed, or succumbed to. Darkness in horror often functions not as aesthetic but as where the threat lives.

**The Three Faces of Fear:**
- **Terror:** The anticipation. Something is wrong. The dread before the door opens.
- **Horror:** The confrontation. The door is open. You see what's inside.
- **Revulsion:** The visceral aftermath. What you saw cannot be unseen. The body's rejection.

Many horror writers recognize these as movements, not choices. Terror builds, horror strikes, revulsion lingers. The common mistake is stopping at any one.

**Core tensions:**
- Safety vs. vulnerability
- Knowledge vs. ignorance (and which is worse?)
- Sanity vs. perception
- The familiar made wrong
- The body as battleground
- Meaning vs. cosmic indifference

**Emotional register:**
- Unease that won't resolve
- Dread that becomes certainty
- Brief hope that makes despair sharper
- Isolation even in company
- The comfort that turns

## Extraction Questions

Weave these into the core phases as appropriate.

### World-Bones Additions

**The Nature of the Threat:**
- "What kind of fear does this story traffic in? Body horror? Psychological unraveling? Supernatural menace? Cosmic insignificance?"
- "Is the threat knowable? Can it be understood, and does understanding help or make it worse?"
- "Does the threat have rules? Can it be stopped, escaped, or only delayed?"
- "Is the threat singular (one monster) or systemic (the house, the town, the world itself)?"

**The Wrongness:**
- "What is normal in this world before the horror arrives?"
- "What's the first sign that something is wrong? Who notices and who doesn't?"
- "Is the horror an invasion, a revelation of what was always there, or a transformation?"
- "What does society know about this kind of threat? What do they refuse to believe?"

**The Rules of Survival:**
- "What behaviors or choices increase danger?"
- "Is survival possible, or only degrees of loss?"
- "What must be sacrificed to escape?"
- "Does knowledge protect or doom?"

### Character Additions

**For Protagonists:**
- "What do they fear before the story begins? What do they fear after?"
- "What makes them vulnerable to THIS specific horror?"
- "What won't they abandon, even when they should?"
- "When do they stop trying to rationalize and start believing?"
- "What's their breaking point? What's beyond it?"

**For Victims:**
- "Why them? Random or chosen?"
- "What moment sealed their fate?"
- "Do they understand what's happening? Is their awareness part of the horror?"

**For Antagonist Forces:**
- "Does it think? Does it feel?"
- "What does it want? Hunger? Purpose? Play? Nothing at all?"
- "Can it be communicated with? Is the attempt part of the horror?"
- "What are its constraints? Every good monster has rules it cannot break."

### Atmosphere Questions

- "What ordinary thing becomes terrifying here?"
- "What senses are most important to this horror? Sight? Sound? Smell? The sense that something is watching?"
- "What is the horror's relationship to light and darkness?"
- "Is there anywhere safe? What makes a space feel safe, and how is that violated?"
- "What time of day/season/weather carries the dread?"

## Trope Catalog

Tropes are tools, not sins. Know which you're invoking and which you're subverting.

### Monster Taxonomy

**The Slasher**
- Human or humanoid, methodical, often masked
- The kills are personal, close, intimate
- What it offers: Primal cat-and-mouse, physical stakes
- Danger: Becomes torture porn without character investment
- Subversion: The slasher has a point; the victims aren't innocent

**The Creature**
- Inhuman biology, often hungry
- Nature without morality
- What it offers: Body horror, survival stakes, the food chain inverted
- Danger: Becomes monster movie spectacle without dread
- Subversion: The creature isn't malevolent—humans invaded its space

**The Ghost/Spirit**
- The unfinished dead, bound to place or purpose
- Often tied to past trauma demanding resolution
- What it offers: Mystery architecture, guilt themes, reality distortion
- Danger: Becomes mystery puzzle that eliminates fear when solved
- Subversion: Resolution doesn't stop it; the ghost was never the real threat

**Cosmic Horror**
- Scale beyond comprehension, entities beyond morality
- Human irrelevance as the horror itself
- What it offers: Existential dread, the collapse of meaning
- Danger: Becomes nihilistic wallowing without story stakes
- Subversion: Characters find meaning precisely because the universe doesn't provide it

**Psychological Horror**
- The threat might be internal, imagined, or gaslit
- Reality itself is unstable
- What it offers: Unreliable narrator, paranoia, identity dissolution
- Danger: "It was all in their head" deflates investment
- Subversion: It IS psychological AND real; both truths coexist

**The Possessed/Infected**
- Something takes over body, mind, or soul
- Loss of self as the horror
- What it offers: Betrayal by your own flesh, watching someone you love become something else
- Danger: Reduces to action once transformation is complete
- Subversion: Consciousness remains trapped, witnessing what their body does

**The Haunted Place**
- House, hotel, ship, town—the setting is the monster
- You can leave (why don't you leave?)
- What it offers: Atmosphere as character, history as threat, no escape from architecture
- Danger: Becomes real estate listing of creepy rooms without character stakes
- Subversion: The place isn't evil—it's protecting something, or containing something

**The Human Monster**
- No supernatural element required
- What people do to each other
- What it offers: No safety in disbelief, "monsters aren't real" offers no comfort
- Danger: Exploitative if trauma is spectacle
- Subversion: The human monster has their own horrors; damage damages

### Structural Tropes

**The Final Girl/Survivor**
- The one who lives to see dawn
- Traditionally: moral, observant, resourceful
- What it offers: POV anchor, earned victory
- Danger: Becomes formula that drains tension
- Subversion: Survival breaks them; victory is indistinguishable from loss

**The Warning Ignored**
- Locals who know, books that shouldn't be read, doors that shouldn't open
- Cassandra function—establishes rules before they're broken
- What it offers: Dramatic irony, rule establishment
- Danger: Characters seem stupid; "just leave" becomes audience refrain
- Subversion: The warning was wrong, or deliberately misleading

**The False Safety**
- The moment of rest that precedes the worst
- Daylight, friends, locked doors—all lies
- What it offers: Rhythm control, contrast that sharpens fear
- Danger: Becomes predictable (safe moment = attack incoming)
- Subversion: The safe moment is actually safe; danger comes from unexpected source

**The Escalation Pattern**
- Each encounter worse than the last
- What was ignored becomes undeniable
- What it offers: Mounting dread, tightening options
- Danger: Escalation requires payoff; peak must be worth the climb
- Subversion: De-escalation is the horror—the thing is getting quieter, closer

**The Investigation/Descent**
- Protagonists seek answers that will destroy them
- Each layer of truth makes it worse
- What it offers: Structure, active protagonists, mystery satisfaction
- Danger: Mystery solving removes fear; explanation deflates dread
- Subversion: The answer doesn't help; knowing makes it stronger

**The Sanity Spiral**
- Reality becomes unreliable through the protagonist's perception
- Can't trust senses, memory, or identity
- What it offers: Reader shares the disorientation
- Danger: Cheap "it was all a dream" deflation
- Subversion: They're sane; the insane explanation is the truth

### Horror Subgenres

**Survival Horror**
- Limited resources, physical stakes, escape as goal
- The monster has rules and can (theoretically) be beaten
- Pacing: Tension, pursuit, respite, renewed pursuit

**Cosmic Horror**
- The threat doesn't care about you personally
- Understanding is the defeat, not the victory
- Pacing: Slow revelation, moment of comprehension, aftermath

**Quiet Horror**
- Suggestion over spectacle
- The thing you don't see
- Dread that never fully resolves
- Pacing: Sustained unease, minimal catharsis

**Body Horror**
- The flesh rebels, transforms, betrays
- Loss of bodily autonomy
- Pacing: Slow wrongness, accelerating transformation, revulsion peak

**Folk Horror**
- Ancient beliefs, rural settings, old ways
- The community as threat, outsiders as prey
- Pacing: Growing isolation, ritual revelation, sacrifice

**Gothic Horror**
- Decay, family secrets, houses with memory
- The past is never dead
- Pacing: Layered revelation, ancestral debt, inheritance of sins

## Style and Voice

### Prose Texture

Horror prose builds through:
- Precision over purple (specific wrong details, not vague spookiness)
- The delay (don't name the threat too soon)
- Sensory wrongness (sounds that shouldn't be, smells that don't belong)
- Short sentences for shock, long sentences for dread
- White space as silence
- What the protagonist doesn't describe (the avoidance that reveals)

**Atmosphere Construction:**
- Establish normal thoroughly; the reader needs to know what's being corrupted
- Let wrong details accumulate before being acknowledged
- Use environment as pressure (too quiet, too cold, too empty)
- Physical discomfort translates to psychological unease

**Avoid:**
- Explaining the monster too early or too completely
- Constant terror without contrast (exhaustion, not fear)
- Jump scares in prose (they don't work without audio/visual)
- Telling the reader to be scared rather than making them scared

### Dialogue Considerations

**Fear in Speech:**
- People rationalize first, describe later
- What they don't say (the sentence that trails off)
- Repetition under stress
- Wrong topics (small talk while something is very wrong)
- When they stop speaking entirely

**The Unreliable Conversant:**
- Testimony that contradicts
- Warnings that don't quite make sense
- Historical accounts with gaps
- The mad survivor trying to explain

**Monster Speech (if applicable):**
- Wrong cadence, borrowed voices
- Knowing things it shouldn't
- Intimacy without connection
- Your own voice from the wrong throat

### Sensory Palette

Horror engages senses for maximum wrongness:
- **Sound:** What you hear before you see it. Sounds from where sounds shouldn't be. Silence with weight.
- **Smell:** Decay, copper, cold stone, wrong sweetness. Memory triggers.
- **Touch:** Temperature drops. Texture of things you shouldn't be touching. Air pressure changes.
- **Sight:** Movement at edge of vision. Wrong scale. Familiar made unfamiliar. What you see when the lights come back.
- **Proprioception:** Being watched. Presence behind you. The sense that the room is smaller than it was.

## Pacing Expectations

### Dread Architecture

Horror pacing follows the breath—long intake, held, explosive release:

1. **Normalcy** — Establish what's at stake, what can be lost. Make the reader care about this world.
2. **Wrongness** — First sign. Dismissed, explained, noted but ignored. The itch.
3. **Confirmation** — It's real. It's here. The rules of normal don't apply.
4. **Escalation** — Each encounter raises stakes. Options narrow.
5. **False Safety** — Breath allowed. Rest that makes the next blow harder.
6. **Revelation** — The truth of what they face. Knowledge that doesn't save.
7. **Crisis** — The worst moment. Maximum exposure.
8. **Aftermath** — Survival or surrender. The cost. Nothing is the same.

### The Reveal Timing

**Too Early:** Monster loses power. Fear needs mystery.
**Too Late:** Frustration replaces dread. Promise must pay off.
**Just Right:** Reveal when the reader can imagine worse than you can show, then show something they didn't imagine.

### Catharsis Management

Horror is unusual—catharsis may be:
- **Denied:** The threat continues, safety never arrives
- **Pyrrhic:** Survival costs everything worth surviving for
- **Corrupted:** Victory is indistinguishable from defeat
- **Traditional:** Threat is overcome, but scars remain

The choice depends on subgenre and theme. Quiet horror denies catharsis. Survival horror earns it.

## Common Pitfalls

**The Explained Mystery**
- Problem: Full explanation removes fear. Knowing exactly what the monster is and why makes it a problem to solve, not a fear to survive.
- Fix: Explanations should raise new questions. Every answer should make it worse.

**The Invincible Villain**
- Problem: If the threat can't be stopped, escaped, or even delayed, reader despairs rather than fears.
- Fix: The monster needs rules. It can be delayed, avoided, temporarily escaped. Hope makes fear possible.

**Jump Scare Dependency**
- Problem: Startling isn't scaring. Prose can't jump-scare effectively anyway.
- Fix: Build dread through accumulation. Earn the shock with anticipation.

**Consequence-Free Violence**
- Problem: Gore without meaning is spectacle, not horror.
- Fix: Every death should cost something. Characters the reader knew, with deaths that mean something.

**Stupid Protagonists**
- Problem: Characters make obviously wrong choices; reader loses sympathy.
- Fix: Make the wrong choice the understandable choice. They split up because they're searching for someone they love. They go into the basement because someone's screaming.

**The Safe Frame**
- Problem: Opening with "here's how I survived to tell this tale" removes all stakes.
- Fix: If using a survivor frame, leave doubt. They may have survived—but as what?

**Darkness Without Light**
- Problem: Constant fear exhausts rather than frightens.
- Fix: Moments of genuine safety, humor, connection—so there's something to lose.

**The Monster That Talks Too Much**
- Problem: Monologuing villain loses menace.
- Fix: Silence is terrifying. Incomplete communication is worse than none. What it doesn't say.

## Monster Design

The heart of horror is the threat. Design with intention.

### Rules Every Monster Needs

**Limitations:**
- What can't it do? Where can't it go?
- These constraints create gameplay (how do protagonists survive?)
- Breaking the rules should be rare and terrifying

**Perception:**
- How does it find prey? Sight? Sound? Something worse?
- This determines how characters hide

**Motivation:**
- Hunger? Rage? Duty? Nothing at all?
- Unknowable motivation can be scariest (cosmic horror)
- Understandable motivation enables negotiation (which may be its own horror)

**Origin:**
- Does it matter? Sometimes mystery serves.
- If revealed, origin should deepen horror, not explain it away
- The tragedy of origin can make monster more terrible, not more sympathetic

### What Makes Monsters Scary

**Wrongness of form:** Things that almost look right. The uncanny valley. Wrong proportions, wrong movement.

**Violation of categories:** Dead but moving. Human but not. Inside spaces meant to be outside.

**Inversion of safety:** The protector becomes threat. The refuge becomes trap.

**Intelligence without empathy:** It's not just hungry—it's interested.

**Reflection:** It shows us something about ourselves we don't want to see.

**Inevitability:** It will find you. It's only a matter of time.

### Monster Voice Modifiers

When extracting antagonist details, add these dimensions:

```yaml
threat:
  # Core elements:

  manifestation:
    physical_presence: "how it appears when seen"
    sensory_warning: "how you know it's near before you see it"
    signature: "what it leaves behind"

  rules:
    limitations: [what it cannot do, where it cannot go]
    triggers: [what summons or activates it]
    weaknesses: [if any exist—and they may not]

  communication:
    - does it speak? how?
    # Examples:
    # - "Mimics voices of loved ones, slightly wrong"
    # - "Silence—the absence of all other sound"
    # - "Speaks in the voice of those it has taken"
    # - "Doesn't speak—you just know"

  motivation:
    hunger: "what it wants from victims"
    purpose: "why it does what it does (if knowable)"
    satisfaction: "does it ever stop? when?"
```

## Example Seeds

Seeds that work well in horror:

- "The shape in the window that wasn't there when you looked away"
- "A voicemail from someone who's been dead for three months"
- "The door that's open every morning, no matter how you lock it"
- "Children's laughter in a house that's been empty for forty years"
- "The family photo where everyone's face is wrong"
- "What the dog keeps barking at in the empty corner"
- "The smell that started when the new neighbors moved in"
- "A room in the floorplan that isn't in the house"
- "The thing you saw in the mirror that didn't move when you did"
- "Your handwriting in a notebook you've never seen"
- "The sound of someone walking behind you that stops when you stop"
- "A name scratched into every window of the old house"
- "The radio station that only broadcasts at 3 AM"
- "What's buried in the garden that the cat keeps digging up"
- "The photograph that keeps appearing in different places"
- "A tooth you find in the wall when you renovate"
- "The bridge nobody crosses after dark, and why"
- "What the previous tenant was trying to keep out"
- "The only channel the TV will play, showing your house"
- "An invitation to your own funeral, postmarked tomorrow"

## Character Depth Extensions

Genre-specific entity fields for character files. The calibrator adds these sections based on active genre modules.

### Extraction Questions

**Fear Architecture:**
- "What terrifies them specifically—not horror in general, but their particular nightmare?"
- "How does fear manifest in their body? Freeze? Flight? Fight? Collapse?"
- "What childhood fear never left them? What new fear did adulthood add?"
- "What's the difference between being scared and being terrified for them?"

**Survival Instincts:**
- "How pragmatic do they get under pressure? What morality survives real danger?"
- "What would they do to live? What line would they cross? What wouldn't they?"
- "Who do they save first—themselves, loved ones, strangers, the group?"
- "What survival choice haunts them? What did they do that they can't undo?"

**Sanity Markers:**
- "How do they track their own grip on reality? What tells them they're still sane?"
- "What do they refuse to believe even when evidence mounts? Where's their denial wall?"
- "What would it take for them to accept the impossible? Or have they already?"
- "When reality breaks, what do they cling to? Logic? Faith? Routine? Another person?"

**Trauma Patterns:**
- "What past wound makes them vulnerable to THIS specific horror?"
- "What did they survive before this? How did it shape their coping mechanisms?"
- "What triggers them—sights, sounds, smells, situations that send them back?"
- "What did fear teach them that's wrong? What survival instinct is actually self-destruction?"

**Darkness Relationship:**
- "What do they do alone in the dark—literal and metaphorical?"
- "What truth comes out at 3am that daylight lets them deny?"
- "What part of themselves do they keep in shadow? What would happen if someone saw it?"
- "Are they more afraid of what's in the dark, or what the dark reveals about them?"

### Examples

**Fear Architecture — Example:**
```yaml
fear_architecture:
  specific_terror: "Being trapped. Small spaces, locked rooms, no escape route. Doesn't matter what's chasing—the cage is the nightmare."
  body_response: "Freeze first. Everything stops—breath, thought, movement. Then flight if freeze fails. Never fight."
  childhood_fear: "The thing in the closet. Still can't sleep facing an open door."
  scared_vs_terrified: "Scared: elevated heart rate, can still think. Terrified: dissociation, autopilot, memory gaps after."
```

**Survival Instincts — Example:**
```yaml
survival_instincts:
  pragmatism_under_pressure: "Principles are luxury goods. When death is real, survival is the only ethic that matters."
  crossing_threshold: "Would sacrifice a stranger to save themselves. Did it once. Still sees their face."
  save_order: "Themselves first. Then the kid. Then whoever's closest. Group comes last. It's ugly but it's honest."
  haunting_choice: "Left someone behind when the door was closing. Hears them screaming in nightmares. Would do it again."
```

**Sanity Markers — Example:**
```yaml
sanity_markers:
  reality_checks: "Counts things. Prime numbers. Recites poems memorized in school. If they can remember, they're still real."
  denial_wall: "Refuses to believe it's supernatural. There's always a rational explanation. Clinging to that even as it crumbles."
  acceptance_threshold: "Saw it kill someone. Can't deny what left that body. Reality is broken now."
  crisis_anchor: "Routine. Same breakfast. Same route. If the world still makes small sense, maybe they're not losing it."
```

**Trauma Patterns — Example:**
```yaml
trauma_patterns:
  vulnerability_wound: "Father was unpredictably violent. Learned to read micro-expressions, anticipate danger. Makes them see threat everywhere now."
  prior_survival: "House fire at twelve. Got out. Little brother didn't. Survival came with guilt they've carried since."
  triggers: ["Smell of smoke", "Locked doors", "Screaming", "Being grabbed from behind", "Enclosed spaces"]
  wrong_lessons: "Learned: staying quiet keeps you safe. Actually: makes you unable to ask for help when you need it."
```

**Darkness Relationship — Example:**
```yaml
darkness_relationship:
  alone_in_dark: "Paces. Can't sit still. Checks locks obsessively. Talks to themselves to prove they're still there."
  3am_truth: "Thinks about dying. Not suicide—just the relief of not having to be afraid anymore."
  shadow_self: "The part that wants to hurt something before it hurts them. The part that understands the monster."
  fear_source: "Afraid of the dark revealing they're not as good as they pretend. That fear makes them cruel. That they'd become the monster to survive."
```

## Integration Notes

**Combine with other modules:**
- **+ Mystery:** Investigation structures the fear; each clue makes it worse
- **+ Thriller:** Adds pace and external antagonist pressure alongside supernatural threat
- **+ Dark Fantasy:** Monster may be understood, but understanding doesn't remove threat (horror with pathos)
- **+ Literary:** Metaphorical horror; the monster represents something (grief, guilt, societal rot)
- **+ Romance:** Gothic romance territory; love complicated by genuine danger (the threat isn't the love interest)

**Extract to:**
- `setting.yaml` → Add threat rules, safe spaces, wrongness markers
- `entities.yaml` → Add `manifestation`, `rules`, `communication` to antagonist forces
- `arc.yaml` → Include dread escalation beats, revelation timing, catharsis type
- `style.yaml` → Add sensory palette, pacing beats, atmosphere construction notes

**Horror/Dark Fantasy Distinction:**
In dark fantasy, the monster can be loved. In horror, the monster can only be survived. The moment you want the monster to win, you've crossed from horror to dark fantasy. Horror doesn't romanticize—it reveals, terrifies, and (perhaps) releases. The goal is fear, not fascination.

## Genre Identity

These are conventions, not commandments. Know the expectations so you can meet or subvert them intentionally.

Tragedy often begins where hope ends—not in despair, but in recognition. The protagonist typically possesses greatness, and that greatness often contains the seed of destruction. We often watch the fall not to witness suffering, but to understand it. The audience frequently knows what's coming before the character does; this dramatic irony is often the mechanism of catharsis.

**Aristotelian Foundations:**
Classical tragedy typically suggests the following patterns:
- Tragedy often concerns persons of high estate (not because peasants don't suffer, but because the fall is often more visible, more consequential)
- The protagonist is frequently neither wholly good nor wholly evil—recognizable, flawed, human
- The fall typically results from hamartia (error in judgment, tragic flaw) rather than vice or depravity
- Through pity and fear, audiences often experience catharsis—emotional purgation, not mere sadness

**Modern Tragedy Extends:**
- High estate becomes any position of meaning (a parent's authority, an artist's vision, a worker's dignity)
- The system itself may be the machinery of doom
- Tragic recognition may come too late—or never
- Catharsis may be bitter, incomplete, or deferred to the audience

**Core Tensions:**
- Fate vs. agency (was this inevitable, or did choices matter?)
- Greatness vs. hubris (where does confidence become blindness?)
- Knowledge vs. action (they see, but cannot stop)
- Individual vs. cosmos (one person against indifferent or hostile forces)
- Dignity vs. destruction (how they fall matters as much as that they fall)

**Emotional Register:**
- Awe more than pity
- Dread more than fear
- Sorrow earned through investment
- Recognition that echoes beyond the page

## Extraction Questions

Weave these into core phases as appropriate.

### World-Bones Additions

**The Shape of Doom:**
- "What forces in this world are larger than any individual will?"
- "Are the gods/fates/systems indifferent, hostile, or simply operating by incomprehensible rules?"
- "What warnings exist that go unheeded? Prophecies, patterns, history repeating?"
- "What is the 'right order' that the protagonist's actions disturb?"

**The Weight of Consequence:**
- "How does this world punish overreach? Through what mechanisms?"
- "What cannot be taken back once done? What lines, once crossed, define the crosser?"
- "How visible is doom to observers vs. the doomed?"
- "What survives the fall? What meaning persists?"

**The Chorus Function:**
- "Who witnesses and comments? Who sees clearly what the protagonist cannot?"
- "What collective wisdom exists that the protagonist ignores?"
- "How does the community respond to greatness? To its destruction?"

### Character Additions

**For the Tragic Protagonist:**
- "What is their greatness? What do they do/possess that elevates them?"
- "What is their hamartia? Not a simple flaw—the shadow side of their strength."
- "At what moment could they have turned back? Why didn't they?"
- "What do they want that they cannot have? What do they not see about why?"
- "When recognition comes, what do they recognize? Just their doom, or something true about themselves/the world?"
- "How do they face destruction? With dignity, denial, defiance, acceptance?"

**For the Surrounding Cast:**
- "Who loves them despite seeing their flaw? Who tries to warn them?"
- "Who is destroyed by proximity to their fall?"
- "Who represents what they could have been? Who represents what they become?"
- "Who speaks the truth they cannot hear?"

**The Hamartia Deep-Dive:**
- "How is the flaw also a virtue? (Ambition that builds empires. Loyalty that blinds. Love that possesses.)"
- "When did the flaw serve them well? When did it begin to betray them?"
- "Is the flaw visible to others but not to themselves?"
- "Could they survive if they surrendered this part of themselves? Would they still be them?"

### Relationship Dynamics

- "Who do they fail through their flaw? Who do they drag down?"
- "What relationship embodies what they're sacrificing for their goal?"
- "Who represents the path not taken?"
- "What love or loyalty do they betray, and how do they justify it?"
- "Who mourns them? Who was right to fear them?"

## Trope Catalog

Tropes are architecture, not ornament. Know which supports you're building on.

### The Tragic Hero

**Classic Form:**
- High estate, great capacity, genuine virtue
- Falls through hamartia, not villainy
- Evokes pity (we suffer with them) and fear (there but for grace...)
- What it offers: Stakes, investment, catharsis
- Danger: Becomes unsympathetic if too arrogant; becomes melodrama if too victimized
- Subversion: The "flaw" is actually the one good thing about them (loyalty in corrupt times, honesty when lies save)

**The Tragic Flaw (Hamartia)**
- Not mere weakness—the excess or misapplication of strength
- Hubris (overconfidence) is the famous one, but many exist:
  - Excessive love → possessive destruction
  - Fierce loyalty → blindness to betrayal
  - Pursuit of justice → vengeance consuming the just
  - Ambition for good → corruption by means
- What it offers: Psychological depth, inevitability, mirror for audience
- Danger: Simplification ("his flaw was anger" is not enough—HOW did anger serve and doom him?)
- Subversion: The flaw is invisible to the protagonist but obvious to everyone else (and the audience)

**Hubris**
- Pride that offends the cosmic order
- Not mere confidence—the belief that rules don't apply
- What it offers: Clear transgression, justifiable doom, moral weight
- Danger: Becomes moralistic finger-wagging; protagonist seems merely stupid
- Subversion: The "hubris" is actually correct—they SHOULD have succeeded; the system is broken

**The Fall from Grace**
- Visible descent from height to ruin
- Must be FALL, not merely failure—requires prior elevation
- What it offers: Dramatic arc, measurable loss, pathos
- Danger: Fall without grace (started low, never mattered) or grace without fall (protected by plot)
- Subversion: They fall UP—lose everything society values, gain what actually matters (still tragic in worldly terms)

### Recognition and Reversal

**Anagnorisis (Recognition)**
- The moment of seeing clearly—truth about self, situation, cosmos
- May come too late to change anything—but changes meaning
- "I was the monster. I was the fool. I was blind."
- What it offers: Emotional climax, meaning in destruction, audience catharsis
- Danger: Too early (then why don't they stop?); too late (never earns it); too explicit (tells us what to feel)
- Subversion: Recognition that changes nothing but HOW they face the end; recognition that the audience has, but the protagonist never achieves

**Peripeteia (Reversal)**
- The turn from fortune to misfortune
- Often when the protagonist's action produces the opposite of intended effect
- The very thing meant to save them destroys them
- What it offers: Irony, inevitability, pattern satisfaction
- Danger: Arbitrary reversal (bad luck is not tragedy); telegraphed reversal (obvious from moment one)
- Subversion: Multiple reversals, each seeming final; reversal that the protagonist engineers knowingly (choosing doom)

**Pyrrhic Victory**
- They achieve the goal; the cost makes it ashes
- What was won is less than what was lost
- What it offers: Bitter complexity, question of worth, ambiguity
- Danger: Victory too complete (not pyrrhic) or too empty (why did they bother?)
- Subversion: They knew the cost and paid it anyway—not blindness but sacrifice

**Noble Sacrifice**
- Choosing destruction for greater good or loved ones
- Death gives meaning where life could not
- What it offers: Dignity in destruction, agency in doom, elevation through loss
- Danger: Sentimentality (too easy, too clean); martyrdom porn (suffering as virtue)
- Subversion: The sacrifice was unnecessary—they misread the situation; or the sacrifice is rejected by those it was meant to save

### Structural Tropes

**The Warning Unheeded**
- Cassandra figures, prophecies, clear omens ignored
- What it offers: Dramatic irony, foreshadowing, audience tension
- Danger: Protagonist seems stupid for ignoring; warning too vague to blame them
- Subversion: They heed the warning and it still happens; heeding the warning causes the doom

**The Nemesis**
- Not a villain—an agent of balancing consequence
- May be a person, force, or fate itself
- What it offers: Externalization of cosmic justice, dramatic confrontation
- Danger: Becomes generic antagonist; too powerful (renders protagonist irrelevant)
- Subversion: The protagonist is their own nemesis; nemesis sympathizes, acts from necessity

**The Tragic Choice**
- Forced between two dooms; no path without loss
- Both options destroy something essential
- What it offers: Character revelation, stakes, inevitability
- Danger: False choice (one option is clearly better); arbitrary stakes
- Subversion: A third option existed but required surrendering what protagonist couldn't

## Style and Voice

### Prose Texture

Tragic prose tends toward:
- Weight without ornamentation—every word earns presence
- Foreshadowing woven into observation (the descriptions are already elegies)
- Rhythm that slows at key moments (the reader should feel the approach)
- Simple language for emotional peaks (grandiloquence retreats; truth is plain)
- The doom-awareness: the prose itself knows what's coming

**Foreshadowing Techniques:**
- Objects introduced with too much attention (the gun before it fires)
- Weather and nature as commentary (summer cannot last)
- Character observations that contain more truth than they know
- The seemingly innocent choice that was the point of no return
- Repeated images that accumulate meaning

**Doom-Awareness in Prose:**
The narrative voice may inhabit dramatic irony:
- "She believed she still had time."
- "He counted his allies, not knowing which were already lost."
- "The morning was beautiful, as mornings in such stories always are."

Or may inhabit tragic consciousness:
- Present tense for inevitability
- Past tense that already mourns
- Conditional constructions: "Had he only..." "If she had known..."

### Dialogue Considerations

**The Protagonist's Voice:**
- Confidence that reads as blindness from outside
- Justifications that the audience sees through
- Moments when they almost SEE—then turn away
- In recognition: simplicity, often questions

**The Warner's Voice:**
- Truth the protagonist cannot hear
- Pleas that the audience knows will fail
- May grow desperate, may give up, may watch in silence

**The Chorus Function:**
- Voice of collective wisdom or fear
- May be a single character, may be community
- Contextualizes action, mourns in advance, questions the gods

### Sensory Palette

Tragedy engages:
- **Time:** The weight of passing hours, the speed of the fall, the stillness before
- **Space:** Heights to fall from, thresholds crossed, rooms that remember
- **Light:** Fading, shadow-lengthening, the brief illumination of recognition
- **Sound:** Silence before the blow, echoes, the last words
- **Weight:** Burdens carried, the heaviness of consequence, the lightness of what's lost

## Pacing Expectations

### The Classical Arc

Tragedy follows a specific shape—the arc bends toward destruction:

1. **Exposition of Greatness** — We must see what will be lost. Establish height before fall.
2. **The Error** — The hamartia-driven choice, often early, often seemingly reasonable
3. **Rising Action (Complications)** — Consequences begin, could still be stopped, protagonist doubles down
4. **The Turn (Peripeteia)** — The reversal, often at moment of apparent success
5. **Falling Action (Descent)** — Consequences cascade, doom becomes visible
6. **Recognition (Anagnorisis)** — They see clearly, too late
7. **Catastrophe** — The fall completes
8. **Resolution/Catharsis** — Meaning extracted from wreckage, order restored or revealed impossible

### Beat Timing

**The Rise Before the Fall:**
- Tragedy requires height—spend time establishing what matters
- The audience must WANT their success before watching their destruction
- Don't rush to doom; earn the investment

**The Point of No Return:**
- A specific moment when outcome becomes inevitable
- May be clear to audience before protagonist
- Often a choice that seems reasonable in context

**The Descending Action:**
- Faster than the rise—once the turn, momentum builds
- Each attempt to fix makes it worse
- Allies fall away, options close

**The Catharsis Beat:**
- Not resolution—purgation
- The audience needs to FEEL the recognition, not just witness it
- Silence after the storm
- What remains when everything else is stripped away

### Tempo Variations

- Slow, heavy passages for weight and dread
- Acceleration as doom approaches
- Stillness at recognition—time stops
- The aftermath: measured, exhausted, clear

## Common Pitfalls

**Grimdark Is Not Tragedy**
- Problem: Suffering without meaning, darkness without depth
- The difference: Tragedy asks "why must this be?" Grimdark says "life is pain"
- Fix: The suffering must illuminate something—character, cosmos, condition
- Tragedy honors its dead; grimdark consumes them

**Unearned Doom**
- Problem: Bad things happen but don't flow from character
- Random misfortune is not tragedy—it's just sad
- Fix: Trace every catastrophe back to the protagonist's choice/nature
- The hamartia must CAUSE the fall, not merely precede it

**No Nobility in the Destruction**
- Problem: Protagonist crumples, whines, or never engages their fate
- Tragedy requires dignity—not stoicism, but engagement with the fall
- Fix: Let them face it. Let recognition be real. Let them meet doom as themselves.

**The Flaw Without the Virtue**
- Problem: Protagonist is just flawed; we never see why anyone loved/followed them
- Hamartia is a virtue turned destructive—we must see both faces
- Fix: Show the flaw working, earning success, being admirable—before it turns

**Melodrama Instead of Tragedy**
- Problem: Emotions unearned, suffering displayed for its own sake
- Melodrama substitutes tears for catharsis
- Fix: Earn every feeling. If they cry, we should have cried first.

**The Villain Did It**
- Problem: External antagonist is the actual cause of downfall
- If the villain is responsible, it's not tragedy—it's defeat
- Fix: The antagonist may be the weapon, but the protagonist must load the gun

**Redemption Escape Hatch**
- Problem: Last-minute save, sacrifice that undoes consequence
- Catharsis requires completion, not escape
- Fix: If they're redeemed, it must cost everything—and not undo the damage

**The Moral of the Story**
- Problem: Tragedy reduced to "don't be proud" or "listen to warnings"
- Moralizing cheapens tragedy; life is not a fable
- Fix: Questions, not answers. Tragedy illuminates without resolving.

## Catharsis Design

Catharsis is not sadness—it is the strange relief of feeling deeply what we normally suppress. Here's how to earn it:

### The Investment Phase

Before destruction, the audience must:
- **Hope** for the protagonist (despite knowing better)
- **See themselves** in the flaw (that could be me)
- **Care** about what will be lost (relationships, achievements, potential)
- **Fear** the visible doom (not shock—dread)

### The Pity Engine

Pity (suffering-with) comes from:
- Recognition of shared humanity ("they're not evil—they're mistaken, like I could be")
- Disproportionate punishment (the flaw is real but the doom is immense)
- Loss of possibilities (what they could have been, now never will be)
- The destroyed innocents (those who loved them, trusted them)

### The Fear Engine

Fear (self-referential dread) comes from:
- The flaw could be mine
- The logic is sound—given who they are, this HAD to happen
- "There but for the grace..."
- The cosmos/system could do this to me

### The Release

Catharsis arrives when:
- We've carried the weight of foreseen doom and finally set it down
- The worst has happened and we survived watching it
- Recognition illuminates—we understand what we witnessed
- The fall reveals something true about existence
- We are purged of pity and fear by having fully experienced them

### Designing Cathartic Moments

- Place recognition at maximum dramatic pressure
- Give the protagonist something to DO with their understanding (even if it's only how they face death)
- Let the language simplify at peak emotion
- Provide witness—someone to receive their final truth
- End not on pain but on clarity (even bitter clarity)

## Example Seeds

Seeds that work well in tragedy:

- "The moment they chose loyalty over truth, not knowing the cost"
- "A promise that cannot be kept without breaking another"
- "The child who trusted them completely"
- "What they built that will outlast them—and remind everyone"
- "The warning they laughed at, years ago"
- "The friend who saw it coming and couldn't stop watching"
- "The gift they gave that became the weapon"
- "The throne they fought for and the person they were before they wanted it"
- "The letter they never sent; the letter they should never have sent"
- "What they whispered to themselves to make it acceptable"
- "The small decision that, looking back, was everything"
- "The last person who believed in them, and when they stopped"
- "The ritual they performed correctly, the god who answered differently"
- "What the mirror shows now versus then"
- "The enemy who understood them better than their allies"
- "The thing they could not forgive in others, because they couldn't forgive it in themselves"

## Character Depth Extensions

Genre-specific entity fields for character files. The calibrator adds these sections based on active genre modules.

### Extraction Questions

**Hubris Architecture:**
- "What's their greatest strength? How does that strength become the flaw that destroys them?"
- "What skill or quality do they lean on too hard? Where's the reliance become rigidity?"
- "What do they trust about themselves that they shouldn't? What blindness does confidence create?"
- "What makes them think they're exempt from the rules, fate, consequences that govern others?"

**Fatal Awareness:**
- "Do they see the fall coming? Fully? Partially? Through a glass darkly?"
- "What warnings do they ignore? Who tries to tell them and why don't they listen?"
- "At what point do they realize the truth? Is it too late? Was it always too late?"
- "How does partial knowledge make it worse? What would full knowledge have changed?"

**Sacrifice Inventory:**
- "What have they already given up to get where they are?"
- "What are they about to lose? What will the fall take from them?"
- "What do they cling to as everything collapses? What's the last thing to go?"
- "After the fall: what remains? Is there anything that survives intact?"

**Denial Patterns:**
- "What truth won't they face? Why? What would facing it cost?"
- "How do they evade reality—rationalization, blaming others, doubling down?"
- "What would force confrontation? What can't be denied anymore?"
- "When denial breaks, what does the recognition look like? What do they finally see?"

**The Thing They Won't Release:**
- "What grip is killing them? What should they let go of but can't?"
- "Is it a person? A principle? A grudge? A dream? A vision of who they are?"
- "Why can't they release it? Love? Pride? Fear? The belief they'd be nothing without it?"
- "Would releasing it save them? Or is the inability to release the nature of their doom?"

## Integration Notes

**Combine with other modules:**
- **+ Romance:** Love as the thing lost, or love as the hamartia. "I would burn the world for you" → and so they do.
- **+ Dark Fantasy:** Immortality as curse, transformation as fall, the monster who was human. What do centuries of life cost?
- **+ Mystery:** The truth they uncover destroys them. The detective who finds what they cannot bear to know.
- **+ Political Intrigue:** The system as doom-engine. One person against structures that will outlast them.
- **+ Horror:** When the fall is cosmic, when the recognition reveals not personal flaw but universal horror.
- **+ War:** The hero whose virtues are vices in peacetime; the leader whose sacrifice saves nothing.

**Hybrid Considerations:**
- Tragic romance: The love cannot save them—may be what dooms them
- Tragic horror: Recognition of what they've become, no way back
- Tragic fantasy: Power as curse, the prophecy fulfilled through resistance

**Extract to:**
- `setting.yaml` → Add fate mechanics, cosmic order, what the world punishes
- `entities.yaml` → Add `hamartia` field, `recognition_arc`, `doom_awareness` to protagonist
- `arc.yaml` → Include recognition beats, reversal points, catharsis design in structure

**The Tragedy Test:**
When checking if the story is tragedy and not melodrama, grimdark, or mere misfortune:
1. Does the fall flow FROM the protagonist's nature? (Not just happen TO them)
2. Is there greatness to fall FROM? (Not just a victim)
3. Is there recognition? (Not just suffering)
4. Does the audience know before the protagonist? (Dramatic irony, not surprise)
5. Is there catharsis? (Meaning extracted, not just pain inflicted)

If any answer is no, the structure needs adjustment.

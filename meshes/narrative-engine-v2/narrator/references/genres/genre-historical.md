# Genre Module: Historical Fiction
# The past as foreign country, period constraints as story engine
# Load alongside core game-maker.md extraction

## Genre Identity

These are conventions, not commandments. Know the expectations so you can meet or subvert them intentionally.

Historical fiction typically makes the dead walk and the forgotten speak. The past often functions not as backdrop—but as character. Every choice, every thought, every possibility tends to be shaped by when and where. Readers may expect to time-travel not just through setting, but through consciousness.

**Core tensions:**
- Accuracy vs. accessibility (how much period authenticity before alienation?)
- Familiar vs. foreign (readers need entry points into alien worldviews)
- Hindsight vs. presence (characters don't know how history "ends")
- Individual vs. era (personal story against historical tide)
- Research vs. story (knowledge serves narrative, never the reverse)

**What historical fiction often offers:**
- Distance that permits examination (examine our present through past's mirror)
- Stakes already proven (we know this world mattered enough to persist)
- Constraints that generate plot (what couldn't be said, done, imagined)
- The thrill of resurrection (bringing the dead back to vivid life)

## Extraction Questions

Weave these into the core phases as appropriate.

### World-Bones Additions

**Locating in Time:**
- "What specific era, decade, or even year? What makes THIS moment matter?"
- "What just happened historically? What's about to? What's in the air?"
- "What major events will occur during your story's timeframe?"
- "What does everyone in this time believe that we now know is wrong?"

**The Shape of Daily Life:**
- "How do people eat, sleep, work, travel? What's the texture of ordinary days?"
- "What technology exists? What's new, what's ancient, what's unimaginable?"
- "How does money work? Who has it? What can it buy?"
- "What do bodies experience differently? Disease, hygiene, physical labor, lifespan?"

**Social Architecture:**
- "What are the rigid hierarchies? Class, gender, race, religion, profession?"
- "What can't be said aloud? What's taboo? What's unspeakable?"
- "Who has power? How is it maintained? Who's invisible?"
- "What are the rules everyone knows but no one writes down?"

**The Mental Landscape:**
- "What do people believe about the cosmos, the soul, the afterlife?"
- "What fears are universal in this time? Plague? Hellfire? Dishonor? Starvation?"
- "What hopes drive people? What does 'success' mean here?"
- "What concepts don't exist yet? What can't be thought?"

### Character Additions

**Period Consciousness:**
- "What beliefs does this character hold that modern readers will find alien or repugnant?"
- "What does this character take completely for granted that we'd question?"
- "What fears or aspirations are specific to this time and place?"
- "How does their class/gender/station shape what they can imagine for themselves?"

**Historical Situatedness:**
- "Where do they fall in the social order? How does that limit them?"
- "What education do they have? What can they read, write, know?"
- "What is their relationship to power, authority, tradition?"
- "What historical events have already shaped them before the story begins?"

**The Modern Reader Bridge:**
- "What makes this character accessible despite temporal distance?"
- "What universal human element transcends period?"
- "Where will readers struggle with this character? Is that productive friction?"

### If Using Historical Figures

- "What's documented? What's invented? Where's the line?"
- "What's the ethical weight of putting words in a real person's mouth?"
- "Are you honoring complexity or flattening into hero/villain?"
- "Whose descendants might read this? What obligations does that create?"

## Trope Catalog

Tropes are tools, not sins. Know which you're invoking and which you're subverting.

### Structural Tropes

**Fish Out of Water**
- Protagonist navigates unfamiliar historical world
- Often: modern sensibilities in past body (time travel, reincarnation, or simply ahead-of-their-time character)
- Offers: reader surrogate, natural vehicle for exposition
- Danger: protagonist becomes smug superiority tourist; "I know better than these primitives"
- Subversion: the "fish" discovers their modern certainties are the limitation

**Hidden History**
- Secret events beneath the official record
- The conspiracy, the cover-up, the forgotten
- Offers: mystery engine, revisionist excitement
- Danger: disrespects actual history; everything becomes da Vinci Code
- Subversion: the "hidden" truth is more mundane than the legend

**Alternate POV on Known Events**
- The servant at the famous dinner, the soldier at the legendary battle
- History from the margins, the unrecorded
- Offers: fresh angle on familiar, dignity to the forgotten
- Danger: actual historical figures become props; marginalized character exists only to witness greatness
- Subversion: the famous event barely matters to our protagonist's actual life

**Witness to History**
- Protagonist present at pivotal moments
- Forrest Gump syndrome: showing up everywhere
- Offers: guaranteed stakes, built-in climaxes
- Danger: strains credibility; protagonist becomes passive observer
- Subversion: they're present but miss the point, or see it differently

**The Secret Modernist**
- Character has suspiciously contemporary values in historical setting
- Offers: reader identification, wish fulfillment
- Danger: historically implausible; suggests past people were simply stupid
- Subversion: their "modern" view has historical precedent (they exist, but were rare and usually suffered for it)

### Constraint Tropes

**Social Prison**
- Character trapped by period expectations (gender, class, race)
- The marriage they must accept, the career they can't have, the love they must hide
- Offers: instant conflict, stakes readers feel viscerally
- Danger: becomes misery tourism; character has no agency
- Subversion: character finds unexpected freedom within or despite constraints

**Forbidden Knowledge**
- Information that's dangerous to possess—heresy, sedition, scandal
- Offers: intellectual stakes, period-specific danger
- Danger: knowledge becomes magic mcguffin
- Subversion: the forbidden knowledge is less important than what pursuing it reveals

**Letters, Diaries, Documents**
- Epistolary elements, found manuscripts, official records
- Offers: period-authentic voice, multiple perspectives, dramatic irony
- Danger: becomes info-dump delivery system
- Subversion: the documents lie, or reveal liar's self-deception

**The Impending Catastrophe**
- Story set before known disaster (Titanic, Pompeii, war)
- Readers know what characters don't
- Offers: exquisite dramatic irony, guaranteed tension
- Danger: becomes disaster tourism; characters exist to die
- Subversion: the expected catastrophe isn't the real story; or they escape and must live with that

### Relationship Tropes

**Cross-Class Romance**
- Love across stations that period forbids
- Offers: guaranteed obstacle, social stakes
- Danger: ignores actual consequences; sentimentalizes inequality
- Subversion: they don't transcend class—class wins, or they find something else

**Historical Romance Conventions**
- Arranged marriage, ruined reputation, marriage of convenience
- Offers: period-specific romantic structures readers enjoy
- Danger: costume drama without consequence
- Subversion: the trope delivers unexpected pain; ruined reputation actually ruins

**Mentor from the Margins**
- Wise figure from oppressed group teaches protagonist
- Offers: challenges protagonist's worldview, gives voice to the silenced
- Danger: Magic Negro / Noble Savage territory; marginalized character exists to educate
- Subversion: the "mentor" has their own story that doesn't center the protagonist

## Style and Voice

### The Dialogue Problem

Period-appropriate dialogue must balance:
- **Intelligibility:** readers need to understand
- **Flavor:** language should feel different from contemporary
- **Flow:** stilted accuracy kills story

**Strategies:**
- Diction and vocabulary choices over grammar reconstruction
- Remove obvious modern slang and idiom without archaic replacement
- Character voice > period voice (a 1850s farmer and 1850s lord sound different from each other, not just from us)
- First-person period narration requires different calibration than third-person authorial

**Avoid:**
- "Thee" and "thou" unless you know the grammar (most don't)
- Phony "forsooth" medieval-speak
- Perfect modern dialogue with occasional "I daresay"
- Dialect written phonetically (almost always condescending)

### Internal vs. External Accuracy

**External accuracy:** props, costumes, settings, events
- Verifiable; readers will fact-check your buttons
- Important but less important than usually assumed

**Internal accuracy:** how people think, what they assume, what they can imagine
- Unverifiable but more crucial
- A character in 1350 doesn't think "this is the medieval period"
- They don't have modern psychology vocabulary
- Their assumed reality is not ours

The internal must be right. The external can be slightly wrong.

### Prose Texture

Historical fiction prose might:
- Echo (without aping) period literary styles
- Use sentence rhythms different from contemporary thriller pace
- Deploy period-appropriate metaphors (nautical for seafaring culture, agricultural for rural)
- Maintain consistent sensory palette (the world smelled different, sounded different)

**Sensory Palette by Era:**
- Pre-industrial: animal smells, wood smoke, limited artificial light, seasonal time
- Early industrial: coal, machine noise, gaslight, clock time
- Early modern: petroleum, electricity, speed, compression of distance

## Pacing Expectations

### How History Shapes Story

**Known Outcomes as Tension:**
- We know Rome falls, the Titanic sinks, the war is lost
- But characters don't—maintain their present-tense uncertainty
- Dramatic irony: we know more than they do; use this for dread, not smugness

**Historical Events as Structure:**
- Major events provide natural act breaks
- The battle, the coronation, the plague's arrival
- But don't let history hijack your story—it provides frame, not plot

**Pacing Differences by Era:**
- Pre-telegraph: information travels slowly; build in delays, uncertainty
- Seasonal rhythms: planting, harvest, sailing seasons shape what's possible
- Travel time: distance is real; journeys take weeks, months
- Let period pace influence story pace

### Tension Architecture

Historical fiction often follows:
1. **Establishment** — the world before disruption (the normal that will be lost)
2. **Incursion** — historical forces enter personal sphere
3. **Adaptation** — character responds to changed circumstances
4. **Crisis** — personal and historical stakes converge
5. **Resolution** — survival, transformation, or loss
6. **Echo** — what remains, what's changed, what this meant

### The Weight of Inevitability

When writing toward known historical outcomes:
- Don't rush toward the disaster—live in the before
- Small domestic moments gain power from what we know is coming
- The characters' hopes are not foolish; they're human
- Grief for the reader, not the character

## Common Pitfalls

**Modern Values Retrofit**
- Problem: characters have contemporary politics, psychology, values
- Fix: give characters historically plausible worldviews, even uncomfortable ones; show how exceptional thinkers were exceptional

**Costume Drama Without Depth**
- Problem: historical setting is backdrop for modern story; swap setting, nothing changes
- Fix: ensure period constraints are load-bearing—remove them and story collapses

**Research Dump**
- Problem: you learned about whaling / sword-forging / corset construction and by God the reader will too
- Fix: deploy research through character experience, not exposition; what characters take for granted, we learn by osmosis

**Protagonist Exceptionalism**
- Problem: character is the one person in 1400 who doesn't believe in God, opposes slavery, supports women's rights
- Fix: even progressive historical figures were of their time; complexity over anachronistic heroism

**Flattening Complexity**
- Problem: historical figures become heroes or villains to serve narrative
- Fix: honor actual complexity; monsters had reasons; heroes had flaws

**Suffering Tourism**
- Problem: historical oppression as entertainment without purpose
- Fix: suffering must serve more than atmosphere; give marginalized characters full humanity, not just victimhood

**Getting the Wrong Things Right**
- Problem: perfect buttons, wrong psychology; accurate slang, impossible thoughts
- Fix: internal accuracy > external accuracy; how they think > what they wear

**The Explanatory Native**
- Problem: marginalized character exists to explain their oppression to protagonist
- Fix: give all characters their own agendas beyond educating others

## Period Authenticity Guide

### What Actually Matters (Hierarchy)

1. **Mindset** — how people think, what they assume, what they can imagine
2. **Social rules** — what can be said, done, transgressed; costs of violation
3. **Material constraints** — what technology allows, what bodies experience
4. **Language flavor** — diction, idiom, register differences
5. **Props and costumes** — visible period markers

Most writers invert this hierarchy. Don't.

### Mindset Calibration

Ask of every character thought:
- Would someone in this time/place/position think this?
- What concepts are they missing?
- What assumptions do they hold that we've discarded?
- What do they believe about why things happen?

**Period-specific psychology:**
- Pre-modern: fate, providence, cosmic order, cyclical time
- Early modern: progress, mechanism, individual will
- 19th century: science, evolution, improvement, empire as natural
- Early 20th: uncertainty, breakdown of certainties, speed, trauma

### Research Integration

**Before writing:**
- Primary sources first (letters, diaries, documents)
- Then secondary (histories, scholarship)
- Then fiction for feel (but don't copy)

**While writing:**
- Research what you need, not everything you might need
- Targeted research > comprehensive research (you're writing a novel, not a dissertation)
- Note contradictions—scholars disagree; you must choose

**When stuck:**
- Return to character, not research
- What would this person do in this situation?
- Research serves story, never the reverse

When extracting character voice (Phase 6b), add these dimensions for historical fiction:

```yaml
voice:
  # Standard fields plus:

  period_markers:
    diction: [vocabulary choices that signal era]

  social_position:
    station: "where they fall in hierarchy"
    education: "what they can read, write, reference"
    mobility: "how fixed or fluid their position"

  belief_framework:
    cosmology: "what they believe about reality, fate, meaning"
    assumptions: "what they take for granted that we question"
    blind_spots: "what they cannot perceive or imagine"

  modern_friction:
    - "where readers will struggle with this character"
    # Examples:
    # - "Accepts class hierarchy as natural order"
    # - "Casual assumptions about gender roles"
    # - "Religious certainty alien to secular readers"
```

## Example Seeds

Seeds that work well in historical fiction:

- "The letter that arrived three months after it mattered"
- "A skill everyone used to know and now no one remembers"
- "The last day before everything changed"
- "What the servants knew and never told"
- "A debt that would take three generations to pay"
- "The trade route that made a family's fortune"
- "Why they built the wall around the garden"
- "The word that didn't exist yet for what they felt"
- "What happened to the first wife"
- "The thing they brought from the old country"
- "A promise made before the war"
- "The year the harvest failed"
- "What they burned when they heard the soldiers coming"
- "The name that was changed, and why"
- "A map that was already wrong when it was drawn"

## Character Depth Extensions

Genre-specific entity fields for character files. The calibrator adds these sections based on active genre modules.

### Extraction Questions

**Period Constraints:**
- "What social rules govern their life? What can't they say, do, or be in this era?"
- "How does their gender, class, race, religion limit or empower them in this time and place?"
- "What freedoms do they lack that they're aware of? What freedoms do they not even know to want?"
- "What would ruin them socially? Economically? Legally? Spiritually?"

**Social Performance:**
- "What's their public persona vs. private self? How wide is the gap?"
- "What mask does this era demand they wear? How well do they wear it?"
- "When are they performing propriety, and when are they actually believing it?"
- "What would happen if the mask slipped? Who would see? Who would care?"

**Forbidden Desires:**
- "What do they want that the era won't permit? Love? Knowledge? Freedom? Power?"
- "How do they pursue it despite prohibition? Secretly? Defiantly? Through proxies?"
- "What's the cost of wanting the forbidden? Reputation? Safety? Soul?"
- "If they could have it, would it be worth what they'd lose?"

**Class Consciousness:**
- "How aware are they of their station? Comfortable? Resentful? Striving to rise or fall?"
- "How do they navigate hierarchy—deference, defiance, manipulation, performance?"
- "What class assumptions do they carry? What prejudices about those above or below?"
- "What would losing their position mean? What would gaining one cost?"

**Temporal Knowledge:**
- "What skills/knowledge are specific to this period that define them?"
- "What daily rituals of the era structure their life? Morning routines? Religious observance?"
- "What do they know how to do that's becoming obsolete? That's newly essential?"
- "How fluent are they in the material culture of the time—clothes, food, tools, transport?"

### Examples

**Period Constraints — Example:**
```yaml
period_constraints:
  social_rules: "Cannot own property. Cannot testify in court. Cannot refuse a husband chosen by father."
  axis_of_constraint: "Woman of middling gentry. Gender closes every door except marriage. Class gives education but not autonomy."
  unfreedoms: "Knows she can't vote, own, or choose. Can't imagine a world where she could. (But beginning to.)"
  ruin_conditions: "Scandal would destroy her. Refusal of suitable match would impoverish her. Speaking her mind publicly would end her."
```

**Social Performance — Example:**
```yaml
social_performance:
  public_vs_private: "Public: accomplished, agreeable, ornamental. Private: sharp-tongued, opinionated, hungry for more."
  the_mask: "The perfect lady. Wears it well because the punishment for dropping it is too high."
  belief_vs_performance: "Performs modesty, doesn't believe in it. Actually believes in duty to family (conflicted about that)."
  slip_consequences: "If the mask slipped: family humiliated, marriage prospects ruined, might be sent away. Aunt would see. Aunt suspects already."
```

**Forbidden Desires — Example:**
```yaml
forbidden_desires:
  what_they_want: "To study medicine. To use their mind for something beyond managing a household."
  how_they_pursue: "Secretly. Reads her brother's medical texts. Attends lectures dressed as a man. It's insane and she can't stop."
  cost: "If discovered: ruin, exile, possibly institutionalized for 'hysteria.' Already risking everything."
  worth_calculation: "Yes. Even if it destroys her. The alternative is dying slowly of boredom and calling it propriety."
```

**Class Consciousness — Example:**
```yaml
class_consciousness:
  station_awareness: "Resentful but pragmatic. Born to middle gentry, not high enough to matter, too high to work honestly."
  hierarchy_navigation: "Performance with superiors (perfect deference). Defiance with equals (subtle). Kindness with inferiors (genuine)."
  class_assumptions: ["Aristocracy: corrupt, useless, powerful", "Servants: more honest than employers", "Merchants: vulgar but free in ways gentry aren't"]
  position_stakes: "Losing station: poverty, labor, freedom (paradoxically). Gaining: more gilded cage, less truth."
```

**Temporal Knowledge — Example:**
```yaml
temporal_knowledge:
  period_skills: ["Court etiquette (hated necessity)", "Herbal medicine (secret passion)", "Proper correspondence (uses to smuggle real thoughts)"]
  daily_rituals: ["Morning: prayers (hollow), dressing (armor)", "Afternoon: calls (performance)", "Evening: needlework (thinks while hands are busy)"]
  obsolescence_awareness: "Letter writing dying to telegrams. Glad. Formal correspondence is a prison."
  material_fluency: "Navigates corsets, calling cards, chaperonage. Knows which fork. Loathes knowing."
```

## Integration Notes

**Combine with other modules:**
- **+ Romance:** period-specific courtship rules, social constraints as obstacle
- **+ Mystery:** historical crime, cold cases, secrets from the past
- **+ Horror:** period fears (plague, witchcraft, damnation), historical atrocity
- **+ Fantasy:** secret history, magic hidden within known events
- **+ Literary:** examines era's meaning, uses past to interrogate present

**Era-Specific Considerations:**
- **Ancient/Medieval:** more research gaps = more freedom; greater cultural distance
- **Early Modern (1500-1800):** rich documentation; dramatic social change
- **19th Century:** most documented; reader familiarity; smallest research margin for error
- **20th Century:** living memory issues; possibly sensitive to descendants

**Extract to:**
- `setting.yaml` → Add era specifics, social rules, material constraints
- `entities.yaml` → Add `period_markers`, `belief_framework`, `social_position` to voice
- `arc.yaml` → Include historical events as structural beats, known outcomes as tension sources
